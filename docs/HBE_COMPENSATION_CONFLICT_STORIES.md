# HBE Compensation Conflict Stories

Purpose: Use concrete stories to test whether a compensation model quietly pushes HBE away from the North Star.

North Star:

> What is best for the buyer?

Revenue-neutral test:

> If HBE earned exactly the same amount regardless of the buyer's ultimate choice, what would we recommend?

These stories are not accusations about individual agents. They illustrate how ordinary compensation structures can create incentives even when everyone involved intends to act ethically.

---

## Story 1 — The More Expensive House

A buyer is deciding between two homes.

Home A costs $285,000.

Home B costs $425,000.

Both could work. Home A meets the buyer's stated priorities better, carries less financial strain, and leaves more room for future choices. Home B is larger, newer, more impressive, and emotionally exciting.

Under a percentage-based buyer-broker compensation model, the brokerage may earn materially more if the buyer chooses Home B.

At 3%:

- Home A produces $8,550 in gross buyer-side compensation.
- Home B produces $12,750.
- Difference: $4,200.

No one has to say, "Buy the larger house because I get paid more."

The conflict exists before anyone behaves badly.

It may subtly affect which strengths get emphasized, how objections are framed, how much enthusiasm is shown, or how quickly financial stretch becomes normalized.

### HBE design response

HBE compensation should not increase merely because the buyer spends more.

Under an agreed professional-fee model, HBE should earn the same amount for materially similar work whether the buyer chooses the $285,000 home, the $425,000 home, another property, or no property.

That makes it easier for HBE to say:

> "The larger house is appealing, but based on what you told us matters, we think Home A may leave you with more of what you actually value."

## Story 2 — The Search That Takes Longer

A buyer hires HBE in April.

They are thoughtful, geographically flexible, and unwilling to buy merely because inventory is available.

By June they have toured several homes and learned that some of their original assumptions were wrong. Their priorities have become clearer, but the right property has not appeared.

Under a traditional transaction-contingent model, the brokerage may have already invested dozens of hours with no revenue earned unless the buyer eventually closes.

The longer the search continues, the stronger the financial pressure becomes:

- encourage compromise;
- frame a merely adequate property as "good enough";
- discourage another round of searching;
- emphasize the cost of waiting;
- normalize buyer fatigue as a reason to settle.

Again, no unethical sentence has to be spoken. The structure itself increases pressure.

### HBE design response

HBE should earn reasonable compensation for professional work as it is performed, rather than making all compensation contingent on producing a closing.

A buyer who learns enough through HBE to decide, "We should wait six months," has not necessarily produced a failed engagement.

If that is the wiser choice, HBE may have done excellent work.

A phase-earned fee structure reduces the economic penalty for giving advice that delays or prevents a transaction.

## Story 3 — The Seller Incentive

Two similar homes are available.

The seller of Home A offers buyer-broker compensation equal to the buyer's agreed HBE fee.

The seller or builder behind Home B offers a materially larger incentive, bonus, marketing payment, or other economic benefit to the broker or agent if the transaction closes.

Home B is not clearly better for the buyer.

If the professional can personally or organizationally keep the additional economic value, the compensation system now favors Home B.

The buyer may never know that two otherwise similar recommendations have different financial consequences for the advisor.

### HBE design response

HBE should disclose seller- or builder-paid compensation and should not allow additional transaction compensation to increase the professional fee beyond what the buyer already agreed to pay for representation.

Where lawful and compatible with the transaction and financing, excess economic value should be handled in a way that benefits or remains with the buyer rather than becoming a hidden reason to favor the transaction.

HBE also does not accept paid referrals, kickbacks, gifts, marketing payments, or other things of value from inspectors, lenders, title companies, contractors, warranty providers, or similar vendors in exchange for steering buyers to them.

## Story 4 — The Ninth Showing Trip

A buyer is searching roughly 53 minutes from the HBE advisor.

They have already made eight showing trips together.

Each trip involves approximately 106 minutes of round-trip travel before local driving, property research, scheduling, and time inside the homes.

The buyer has become more discerning because the process is working: they now understand their tradeoffs better and are ruling out homes for more thoughtful reasons.

But the traditional transaction model makes another showing trip economically painful for the agent. There is no additional compensation for trip nine, ten, or eleven unless a closing eventually occurs.

That can create pressure to say:

- "This is probably the best you're going to find."
- "You may be getting too picky."
- "At some point you just have to choose."

Sometimes those statements could be useful truth. But a compensation system that makes additional learning costly to the professional makes it harder to know whether the advice is genuinely buyer-serving.

### HBE design response

HBE compensation should acknowledge real professional workload without turning every showing into a meter that makes the buyer afraid to ask for service.

The preferred model is a clearly defined professional-service scope that includes a realistic amount of search and showing work, with transparent extension pricing if the scope becomes materially larger than expected.

The extension should be tied to additional workload, not to the price of the home and not to whether the buyer ultimately closes.

This preserves two truths at once:

1. HBE's time and professional capacity are real and valuable.
2. HBE should not become financially better off merely because the buyer spends more or closes sooner.

# What These Stories Teach Us

A compensation system should be tested for at least four distortions:

1. **Spend more** — Does HBE earn more if the buyer buys a more expensive home?
2. **Close sooner** — Does HBE become economically worse off when the buyer needs more time?
3. **Choose the incentivized property/provider** — Can outside compensation affect HBE's economics?
4. **Stop learning** — Does additional diligence, showing, or reconsideration become financially painful enough to create pressure toward completion?

The goal is not to create a business in which HBE's time has no cost.

The goal is to make the cost of professional representation depend primarily on the professional service required — not on the buyer's purchase price, emotional state, seller incentives, or willingness to close.

# Public Storytelling Recommendation

These should eventually become 2–4 plain-language public articles or expandable stories rather than one technical compensation page.

Possible titles:

- **Why Should Your Agent Earn More Because You Spend More?**
- **What Happens When the Right Answer Is "Not Yet"?**
- **Who Benefits When a Seller Offers Your Agent More Money?**
- **Why Thoughtful Buyers Shouldn't Become Expensive Problems for Their Agent**

Each story should end with:

> The existence of a conflict does not mean a professional will act badly. It means a good system should not require good intentions to overcome avoidable incentives.

And then explain how HBE handles that specific conflict.
